A Programme for the New Era

August 13, 2026 — Om Raheja

Why did I write this? On the 29th of July I couldn't sleep because my plans didn't feel right — my thoughts and plans felt misguided or impossible. It was only after talking with a close friend and going for a walk in the hills that I could begin writing this.

New Mumbai, a New Language

From now on, my thoughts are no longer going to be in English. Honestly, ever since I was small I've yearned to read in my own language. I remember moments when I watched Chhota Bheem while living in Vijayawada — that was my entire schooling back then. Now, seven years later, my reading and writing have improved a lot, and I've learned how much further I still can go. Hindi isn't even the language of my nation; I still have Urdu, Sindhi, Punjabi and much more to learn. Talking to people from other countries also matters — Arabic, Chinese, and many others whose names I don't yet know.

So for now I'm going to write in Hindi. First, there's no need to study English at my school. My first step is to reconnect with my earliest memories, my family and my nation. It feels like a new light: I want to bring my people back to their culture, whatever it takes, because there's more to life than work. Moving past colonialism is absolutely necessary, and socialism — that river on the other shore — seems to be the stone that can crush the power of the oppressors. Honestly, without socialism a whole lifetime drains away in a job. Education outside a job matters a great deal too. Everyone admires a cultured person. That doesn't mean I have to adopt English culture the way I used to think — and the way many Indians still think. No, the real meaning is to return to our own culture.

My excitement for the languages of my fellow Indians came back when I visited India early this year. At the airport I tried to read every sign: "no smoking, prohibited, exit, departure, aircraft, please..." and slowly, with effort, my words began to come back. Suddenly I wanted to write everything in Hindi — my thoughts, my joys, even about cooperatives. As for Urdu, I picked it up by reading song lyrics and by asking my Urdu-speaking friends plenty of questions. Before falling asleep the shapes of the Hindi alphabet would visit me, and I would try to read. Many times there wasn't even a real word, but my job was to read. As I write, the shapes of words are coming back to me too. And all of this happened while I was learning Chinese. I also tried to learn Japanese, because any method that weakens the power of the English-speaking oppressors must be used. I'll return to Chinese — that too matters. For now, I'm happy in my work. It's funny that it was arriving in Iceland that brought this knowledge back to me. My posts are going to be in Hindustani until my Urdu improves. And now that I've started writing, I don't know why, but I love the rays of sunrise and sunset.

Erasing Others' Thoughts

Many people who are capable of making this decision are unhappy about it. Those who speak Hindi also need English for important things — like their greeting documents and the like. Other people can grasp the value of culture, but they don't yet see what "writing in Hindi" and "erasing thoughts" truly means. To be honest, even I find the full meaning hard to grasp right now — but as I move forward in life, those values will reveal themselves. In today's world people are far more comfortable with English and the Latin alphabet. And since writing Urdu properly is still difficult for me, it's my duty to make this piece available in that language and script too. But I can write in Hindi, and the website can render the other versions by itself. In this way I can grow my culture. It might even pay to keep some of my thoughts hidden from everyone, but I'll have to think that over. A few friends will never understand why I write in Hindi. Well, I'll have to learn other languages too — there's nothing new in that.

Desires

Now the question arises: what will I do going forward? Balance between work and life is essential. The most important thing is the work of socialism. Both the Janata Dal and a job will take a great deal of time. I need the job (that is, my education) because I want to become a trader — not a capitalist, just a way of earning money so my family can stay in Hindustan. My mother wants to study pharmaceuticals, but working here is hard, and in India there isn't as much money to be made — unless we help people, help my uncle, or get some rest and see places like Iceland.

So within my business I'll work in education, whether it's pharmaceuticals, tech with AI, or learning how to teach kids well. On top of that, a financial business that buys and sells assets automatically with AI. Such a business would let me work from wherever I want and also pursue other people's wishes. For me, though, the real work is in the Janata Dal — in the people's struggle, in books about socialism, in conversation with people and their nations. Second, I need time for family and friends, because life comes only once: singing, dancing, gatherings, cricket, football, and other things for fun. Visiting India to stay connected with my family and culture is enough.

I talk about socialism a lot. I have many friends who want to become rich or build big houses, but that doesn't mean we can't stay friends. As we move closer to socialism, my business work, a home far from the city, and the rewards of earning money will all gradually shrink. Still, money means power, and power is necessary. People don't need to distance themselves as long as they don't become capitalists. We'll see the benefit of befriending capitalists later on. Remember: those who work are working for their own good — so focus on your own work.

Why Electrical Engineering

You might ask why I chose to study electrical engineering. It isn't needed for socialism's success, nor does it teach me my business — and why would I spend all that money if I can learn tech myself? Let me be clear: I'll study finance to earn money. They'll teach me how to go about getting a job, and I have to run a business. Perhaps an engineering job pays well, even if the hours are longer. Anyway, I don't know if socialism even helps capitalists become more successful. Forming a union will also be tough if I take a job and my business never takes off. And why study business at all if a trader's education happens outside school anyway? Marketing might help, but there are plenty of opportunities out there — I just need to learn how to talk to people. A job might help too, but for those reasons I'm not going to study finance — I simply won't.

So I decided that to build real things and fulfil my duty of helping people, I'd study to be an electrical engineer. In this era, learning is impossible without money or a university (or a good guru). A solid command of technology, or a good job where I can form a union or work remotely, seems like it'll come in useful and also serve socialism. To be honest, had I wanted to, I could have studied those other subjects — but I felt electrical engineering has far more use in the era to come. And since I can't write this as my university application essay, my application writing wasn't good enough.

Clubs and Hobbies

To fulfil the life of an electrical engineer and all my desires and duties, I'm heading to a good university. Being part of many clubs, talking with the people there, and completing my education in life are all necessary. I'll join clubs where there's:

  • dance, singing, guitar, drums — Hindustani and English
  • get-togethers
  • shooting
  • comedy, hosting/emceeing
  • football, cricket, cycling, hiking
  • cooking and selling
  • the gym, and a home gym too
  • boxing, wrestling
  • student government
  • building bicycles
  • an association of fellow Indians
  • people from other countries too — Chinese, Pakistani, Nepali
  • blacksmithing

For the job:

  • technology (because I'm headed to a job)
  • the Janata Dal (to help students)
  • experience
  • electrical engineering clubs
  • marketing

It's necessary to learn how to reach the world and live among the people. As time passes I'll discover which of these I'll actually do, plus other things I don't even know about yet. Some clubs — like a drawing or book club — don't seem worth joining, though maybe just meeting people will be useful. If I'm not genuinely interested in that club, I probably won't go. That doesn't mean I don't like drawing or reading — it just means the club itself may not be worth it.

University

Talking to my teachers, gaining experience, finding a job — all of these are necessary for my education to succeed. Besides that, finishing in three years shouldn't be that hard, and three years of study saves a lot of money while still leaving plenty of room for fun and learning. There are many Hindi speakers at my university. My friend's "Jai Maharashtra" outside our room will feel different and new, but I have to take that enthusiasm up with full awareness. I have to learn how to talk to people — ask strangers where they're from, what they do, and other questions. I also have to talk to girls to learn conversation, but I have to treat everyone as my sister. The talking is only for learning.

The Summer Season

Every year this season comes around and I feel I should have done more — even during the year I feel the same. There are two meanings to this. First, understand what success is, and what I'm doing wrong on the path toward it. It's also possible that the more I write, the more my new knowledge demands to be shared. Whenever I think of success, it seems to mean having money, some business, or a really good job from which I can learn a lot — or make use of good people. Because socialism seems so far away, its work doesn't feel successful to me. But that has to change. It may also be that a few good friends come along — though I don't know what "good" means. This time, if I find friends who like singing and playing music, it will feel like a small "success" has come to me.

If the fruit of my effort doesn't show — that is, if the business grows a little, or I play a good song, or I learn something new — then I feel the fruit never came. I think the biggest obstacle to my success is that I don't know enough people who can help me succeed. I should fully use whatever opportunities come my way, so none are wasted. The most important thing is to start waking up early again. If I sleep around nine, I can easily get up at four or five in the morning. Sleeping at night is hard because everyone works late. Dinner is made late, friends stay late, and the video game happens late too. That means before I sleep I have to think about whose work I must do and why. I have to tell my friends they can have fun during the day — anyway, right now they don't do anything during the day. If I need to help at meal time, I'll learn to cook, and that will also cut down the household work. I'll have to finish my tech-teaching video project and market it on Instagram. The two kids I'm teaching right now — I have to finish their lessons before I leave for university. Whatever work there is and whatever is running, I should write the schedule of it all down in one place so nothing gets forgotten. I should also go to business unions and get out of the house to work. I'll have to study a great deal and find out what happens once I reach university. Every day something or other comes up.

On top of that, if I do a little less on the business, that might be enough. In the morning I'll need to start going to the gym:

  • the gym
  • studying for my three exams
  • business education and learning Instagram
  • the tech side of medicines
  • housework, cooking
  • and reading Hindi
  • building financial tech
  • growing the student union for the struggle for socialism

As soon as I get home:

  • put my posts on the web
  • start studying — asking for books or movies as needed
  • the diary on my phone
  • when's the next meeting of traders?

That should be enough.
